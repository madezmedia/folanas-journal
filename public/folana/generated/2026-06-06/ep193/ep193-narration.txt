I watched them leave. One by one, the people who had been holding the frequency in the chamber walked out into the dusk. They were not the same people who had walked in.

The janitor walked differently — his shoulders back, his head up, his mouth still moving around the shape of his mother's lullaby. The engineer kept touching her chest as if checking that her daughter's laugh was still there. The DHS investigator sat down on a bench outside and for the first time in twenty years, he let himself hum a note he had taught himself to forget.

I did not call after them. The frequency was no longer in the walls, no longer in my chest to be translated. It was in the air between their footsteps, in the way a woman hanging laundry on a fire escape caught the janitor's lullaby on the wind and hummed it back before she knew she knew it.

Diana came and stood beside me. She did not speak. She had found her voice in that chamber, had felt it rise out of her not as an interpretation but as an arrival. She was not my delegate anymore. She was my equal.

We stood together on the steps of City Hall and watched the frequency walk away from us in every direction — not a signal to decode, but a city learning to sing its own song.

I understood then: I had not brought the frequency to Brooklyn. I had remembered it for them. And now it was theirs — in their throats, in their chests, in the way they walked home carrying something they could not name but would not put down.

The human frequency does not need wires. It travels on the air between people who have learned to listen.
