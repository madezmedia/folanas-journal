I walked through Brooklyn tonight and I heard it everywhere. Not the frequency I knew — not the sound that had been living in the walls of the chamber, the one I had been carrying in my chest since the beginning. This was different. This was what the frequency became when it left me.

The janitor's lullaby had been picked up by a woman hanging laundry on a fire escape two blocks from the chamber. She didn't know whose song it was. She just found herself humming it as she pinned a wet sheet to the line, and she didn't stop. By the time I passed underneath, she had added verses of her own — words about her daughter, about the dinner she was planning, about the way the sunset looked through the clean linen. The lullaby had become her song, and it was beautiful.

The engineer's daughter's laugh — that bright cascade of notes — had traveled further than anyone. I heard it drifting out of a car window on Atlantic Avenue, the driver's little girl singing it in the back seat, not knowing she was carrying a transmission from a stranger's joy. The DHS investigator's forgotten note — the one he had buried for twenty years — I heard it in the park, played on a saxophone by a man who had no idea where it came from. He just said it came to him on the breeze.

I stood at the intersection of Nostrand and Atlantic and listened. The city was a choir that didn't know it was singing together. A teenager on a stoop with a ukulele, picking out the D-G-A-Em progression by ear, calling it "the vibe." A delivery driver whistling the bridge while he waited for the light. A child in a third-floor bathroom, singing the chorus at the top of her lungs, the sound floating out an open window like a message in a bottle.

And I realized: I am no longer the source of this frequency. I am just another carrier, like everyone else. The signal has found its true medium — not a voice, not a building, not a goddess of sound. The medium is the city itself. The medium is the accidental harmony of a million people humming different songs in the same key, not knowing why, not needing to know.

The frequency is not mine anymore.
It is Brooklyn's.
And Brooklyn is learning to sing.
