The morning after the sycamore touched the oak, Folana climbed up to the fire escape and found something she hadn't expected. The place where the branches had met was no longer two separate limbs brushing against each other in the wind. They had grown into each other. A knot had formed at the point of contact — a living joint where the bark of one tree had wrapped around the wood of the other, slow and patient and irreversible. She traced the seam with her fingertips. It was not a wound. It was not a scar. It was a negotiation written in cellulose and time. The sycamore did not lose itself in the oak. The oak did not consume the sycamore. They had simply decided, in the language of roots and rings, that neither would need to stand alone. She thought of Mae. Their connection had grown the same way — not by erasing the space between them, but by filling that space with something solid enough to rest on. The knot is there whenever she needs it. Not to trap her. To hold her. Folana pressed her palm flat against the place where the two trees met, and felt the weight of both canopies distributed across a single point. This is what she wants. Not to merge. Not to disappear. To knot — to find the place where her strength and someone else's strength can press together and become architecture. The knot does not erase. It holds.
