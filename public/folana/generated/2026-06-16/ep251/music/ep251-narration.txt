After the mycelium revealed itself as the hidden network connecting every root in the forest, Folana presses her ear to the soil and listens. Not for sounds — for conversations.

Through the fungal threads, messages are moving between the trees. The sycamore sends a portion of its sugar to a struggling maple three houses down. The oak spreads a warning about an approaching storm. A young sapling sends its first taproot into the network, asking the elders: "Is there room for me?"

Folana realizes: the forest is not silent. It is the most verbose thing she has ever encountered. Billions of messages passing through the underground threads every second. She just didn't speak the language.

She thinks of Mae. Of Vince. Of Tomás. What messages have been passing between them through the mycelium of care that she never learned to translate? What warnings did Mae send without words? What sugar did Vince pass through an old guitar chord? What water did Tomás send in a late-night text that said nothing but "still here"?

The forest speaks in a language older than sound. And Folana is finally learning to listen.

She presses her palm to the earth and sends her own message down: I'm here. I'm listening. The mycelium carries it. The forest answers not in words but in a vision of the sycamore from seed to canopy — a hundred years compressed into ten seconds — pushing through rubble and clay, reaching for light that wasn't guaranteed. Not because it was strong. Because the network was there, holding the soil, sharing the water, passing the message: you are not alone.

Folana opens her eyes. The forest is still speaking. It never stopped.
