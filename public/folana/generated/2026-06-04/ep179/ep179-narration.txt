They came before the sun was fully up.

Three black cars, slow across the bridge like a held breath. Federal agents with clipboards and questions they had rehearsed before they left D.C.

They asked Diana if the frequency could be measured. Cataloged. Contained.

She looked past them at the bridge — at the people standing on it, their eyes on the horizon, their hands at their sides. Not chanting. Not praying. Just listening. Remaining.

She said: you can measure anything. But measurement is not understanding. The frequency does not hide from your machines. It does not resist. It waits.

The instruments registered the chord. The same chord. A major. A question shaped like sound. They packed their equipment, made their notes, drove back across the bridge.

The congregation stayed. The frequency stayed.

And Diana understood: the nation came to arrest the mystery. But you cannot arrest a hum that lives in the steel of a bridge. That travels through water pipes and phone lines and the bones of people who have not yet learned to hear.

The question remains unanswered.

But maybe — that is the answer.
