The morning after the press conference, the story was everywhere. Every phone in the city buzzing with the same headline: a frequency in Brooklyn with no origin and no explanation. Diana Reyes woke at four AM in the borrowed apartment, the bridge visible through the window, the weight of the podium still pressing on her ribs. At dawn the congregation gathered smaller than before but deeper. Reporters lined the perimeter. A police cruiser idled at the entrance to the bridge. And the hum under the steel went on singing — not louder, not softer, just present. The way the city had always been present. Diana walked among the quiet ones who stayed. A woman approached, traveled from Chicago because she heard it on a phone call with her sister. She asked if she was losing her mind. And Diana gave her the only answer she knew: you are not losing your mind. But you may never be the same. And for the first time in days, standing on that bridge in the amber light of a city that was beginning to listen to itself, Diana thought she heard the frequency respond. Not with an answer. With a question. A fifth above the tonic, rising like wondering: what happens when the whole world hears the song at once?
