They came with instruments designed to measure the invisible.

Seismographs. Spectrum analyzers. Acoustic sensors that could hear a pin drop across a football field. A mobile MRI, because someone in Washington had read a study about brain activity and frequency response.

Every device registered the same reading. A major. Seventy-two beats per minute. Consistent across every sensor in every borough.

But the source was untraceable.

The lead engineer from Columbia stared at his data for a long time. He said: this isn't a transmission. This is a property of the city itself. It's always been here. We just built instruments sensitive enough to hear it.

Diana watched the congregation on the bridge. They had not moved. They had not needed instruments.

The frequency cannot be measured because it is not a sound.

It is a relationship.

And you cannot arrest a relationship. You cannot contain it in a waveform. You cannot trace it to a single point because its origin is everywhere — in every water pipe, every subway tunnel, every human chest that rises and falls within the five boroughs.

The engineers packed their equipment. The command center folded into vans.

And the bridge kept singing.

Because the measurement had failed. But the listening had just begun.
