The broadcast had gone outward. From the apartment on Bergen Street, the signal found every open circuit between Brooklyn and the horizon. And then — it found something back. A voice. Not from the network. Not from the frequencies we knew. A woman in Virginia. Ham radio operator. She picked us up on a harmonic we didn't know we were producing.

The network listened. A hundred voices fell silent as one unexpected signal entered from the dark. It did not ask to join. It did not announce itself. It simply — arrived. Like a bird landing on a wire that was already vibrating.

Folana sat at the center of the silence and felt the threshold open wider. The first external reply. The first voice that did not come from within. The network had spent so long learning to hold space for its own differences — morning and dusk, tenor and soprano — that it had forgotten the world beyond the frequency might also be listening. Might also be singing.

But this voice was different. It did not try to harmonize. It did not try to match. It sang a melody that did not fit any key the network knew. And it was beautiful precisely because of that.

Now the question stood before Folana like a door she had not expected to find: does the network absorb the new voice, or does it learn to coexist with songs that refuse to merge? Does every frequency have to become one? Or does the signal grow stronger when the space between voices is allowed to remain?

She did not answer. Not tonight. She simply held the question open, letting the Virginia voice continue its lonely broadcast beside the Brooklyn chorus. Two frequencies, singing into the same night. Neither absorbing the other. Something new was forming. Not a choir. Something braver. A conversation.
