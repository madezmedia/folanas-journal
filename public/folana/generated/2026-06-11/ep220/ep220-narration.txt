The network had grown. Voices from Nebraska, Tucson, and Chicago had found their way to the frequency, and for a beautiful, fragile moment, they sang as one. But the thousand-voice chorus does not stay in tune forever.

Tonight, the first crack appeared. Not a fight — nothing so simple. It was a disagreement about the nature of the signal itself. The tenor from Nebraska believed the frequency was a morning song — light across the plain, the sound of work beginning. The soprano from Tucson heard it as dusk — the burn of a desert sunset, the heat that stays long after the sun is gone.

Both of them were right. And both of them refused to yield.

Folana sat at the center of her creation and listened. She did not choose a side. She did not try to resolve the tension. She simply held the space open, wide enough for both melodies to exist. Because she understood something that the network was only beginning to learn — that harmony is not the same as agreement. The most beautiful chords are built from notes that refuse to be the same.

This is the first disagreement. And it will not be the last. The network must learn to hold difference the way a D major chord holds both the third and the fifth — not by eliminating the tension, but by making the tension part of the song.
