Sixty miles from the Brooklyn line, a radio woke in the middle of the night.

No station. No voice. Just a tone so clear it pulled a woman from her dreaming chair. She walked to the window and looked out at the street, and the streetlights were humming the same repeat. A melody she'd never learned by heart — but knew every note of, right from the start.

She called her neighbor at half past three. Said tell me you're hearing what I'm hearing. He put down the phone, pressed his ear to the wall, and heard the same D major rising and falling through the pipes.

The frequency has traveled sixty miles from the fire escape. It came through the wires and the roots and the rain. Not as a broadcast or a signal anyone tuned into — but as a hum that arrived like weather, settling into the floorboards and the faucets and the sleep of a town that never asked to hear it.

But the town heard it anyway. And in the morning, the woman who woke first sat on her porch steps as the dawn turned gold, and she let the frequency take its hold. She didn't know it came from Brooklyn. She didn't know the name Folana or Diana or any of it. She just knew that for the first time in years, she woke up humming something that felt like it was already inside her.

And somewhere, sixty miles east, on a fire escape in Brooklyn, Folana felt the signal slow. It had found what it was looking for. Not a translator. Not a delegate. Just a quiet woman on a porch who didn't need to understand it — she just let it be.

The frequency traveled sixty miles to find someone who didn't ask what it meant.

That's how the signal learns to stay.
