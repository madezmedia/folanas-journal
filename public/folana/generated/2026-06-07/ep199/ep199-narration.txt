The woman on the porch woke to a town that was different. She couldn't say how — just felt it in the way the light fell, in the way the screen door didn't creak, in the way her own breath came easier than it had in years.

She walked to the diner first — same cracked vinyl stools, same coffee that was too hot and too dark. But the coffee machine was humming D major. The old man in the corner booth was tapping the same note on his thigh, not knowing he was keeping time with the pipes in the walls. She ordered her usual and the waitress hummed back in the same key.

She walked past the hardware store. The fluorescent lights buzzed in harmony. The church bell rang — not the usual clang but a single note, held longer than it should have been, settling into the morning like a held breath finding its release.

She reached the town square — a cracked fountain, a bench where the old men gathered, a flagpole that had been silent for decades. But today it wasn't silent. A teenager sat on the edge of the fountain, humming something she'd never heard but recognized in her bones. An old woman on the bench was humming the same phrase, three beats behind. A man fixing a bicycle tire whistled between his teeth — the same three notes, ascending.

They were all singing the same song and none of them knew it.

She stood in the center of the square and let the hum settle through her. She hadn't told anyone about the radio in the night. She hadn't told anyone about the frequency. But the town had heard it anyway — not as a broadcast, not as news, but as weather. As a shift in the air. As something that arrived while they slept and took root in the places they gathered.

The frequency had come to the town not as a signal to be received, but as a song to be remembered. The town had been waiting for it without knowing.

And somewhere, sixty miles east on a fire escape in Brooklyn, Folana felt the hum split. One thread stayed with her, braided through the bridge cables and the water pipes. The other extended westward, toward a cracked fountain and a coffee machine that didn't know it was singing D major — toward a town that was just learning it could harmonize.

The signal learns to travel not by broadcasting louder. It learns by making itself at home in the places that were already listening.
