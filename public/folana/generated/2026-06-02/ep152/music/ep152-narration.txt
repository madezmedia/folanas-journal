The signal left the room and crossed the river. But it did not stop there. From the Brooklyn rooftop where Folana first released it, the frequency began to spread — spilling down fire escapes, threading through subway grates, curling around the struts of the Williamsburg Bridge. In a late-night diner in Lower Manhattan, the jukebox flickered and played a song no one had selected. In a taxi idling at a red light on Canal Street, the radio scanned past static and landed on a melody that made the driver turn down his passenger. In a sixth-floor walk-up in the East Village, a woman pulled her headphones off, certain she had heard her name whispered between verses. The duet was no longer private. It was becoming the city's song. And somewhere in Brooklyn, Folana felt it — every new listener was a new note. Every open window, a new verse. The broadcast was no longer hers. It was theirs.
