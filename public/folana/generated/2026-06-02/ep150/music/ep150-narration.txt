Two voices. One frequency. That's what it became. Not a broadcast anymore. A duet.

I sang from Brooklyn. She answered from Manhattan. Not in words — I don't know her name, don't know her face. But I know her voice. It arrived wrapped in the signal I sent out last night, folded around my frequency like paper around a note.

Three fast. A pause. One long. She learned the pattern before I taught it to her.

The first time I heard her sing back, I was on the fire escape watching the East River glitter between the buildings. It started as a hum — low, uncertain, like someone testing whether a microphone is on. And I felt it in my chest before I heard it in my ears. The signal had crossed back, carrying a passenger.

I closed my eyes and answered. A C-sharp, held until the vibration settled into the space between us. She caught it on her side. Held it. Bent it a quarter-tone sharp and then let it fall into a harmony I hadn't planned.

We sang for hours. Not songs. Just notes finding each other across the water. Her voice was deeper than I expected — a warm alto that felt like the bass of a faraway piano. She would offer a phrase, I would mirror it, bend it, send it back changed. We were passing a frequency back and forth like a conversation, except we were speaking a language that predates words.

The streetlamp flickered in our rhythm. Three fast. A pause. One long.

Somewhere between midnight and the first fringe of dawn, I stopped being Folana-the-broadcaster and became Folana-the-singer. Because broadcasting means speaking to no one in particular. But singing...

Singing means someone is listening.

And somewhere across the river, in a city of eight million, the someone who heard me was humming back a harmony I would have never found alone.

Two voices. One frequency. The Frequency Broadcasts were never about transmission. They were about this — the moment when the signal is no longer a message, but a meeting.
