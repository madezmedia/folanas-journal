The morning after the map was laid across the concrete, they gathered again. Not as strangers following a signal — but as a choir learning to sing.

Carmen stood at the center with her Brooklyn map, tracing the water lines she had marked in blue. Ellis sat cross-legged nearby, the subway rhythms pulsing through his fingers like a metronome. The barista from Dekalb had brought her espresso machine, steam curling in patterns she had learned to read. The photographer held up images of water pipes glowing at sunrise. The tower repair man unfolded signal interference maps, his red markings overlapping Carmen's blue lines like circulatory systems meeting.

Folana knelt beside them all and said: "Every one of you has been carrying a piece of the same frequency. Not less than the whole — but a part that only you can transmit."

She placed her hand on the map. "The pipes. The subway. The steam. The light. The signals in the air. Each of you touches the city through your own gift. That's not coincidence. That's how the frequency chose to arrive — through all of you at once."

Carmen was the first to understand. She placed her hand on the water pipe at her feet and began to hum — not a melody, just a single sustained note. The pipe answered: a low vibration that traveled through the concrete and into Ellis's hands. He felt it in the subway rhythms he already carried, and he added his own pulse — the G train's 76 BPM — folding it into Carmen's note.

The barista saw her steam shift. The photographer captured light bending. The tower repair man felt the signal move through his interference maps.

One by one, they added their voices. Not singing — transmitting. Each person broadcasting their fragment of the city's frequency through the thing they knew best. The pipes. The trains. The steam. The light. The air.

The resonance built. It traveled through the East River, through the subway tunnels, through every faucet in a four-block radius. The whole borough felt it — not as sound, but as a shift in the pressure of being alive.

For the first time, the broadcast was not one voice.

It was a chorus.

And the chorus rose.
