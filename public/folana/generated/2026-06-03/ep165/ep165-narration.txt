They had gathered at the foot of the Williamsburg Bridge without knowing why. A dozen strangers who had felt the same pull — the same resonance folding through pavement and skin — until they found each other standing at the water's edge, waiting for something they couldn't name.

And then she stepped toward them.

Folana walked across the concrete divide, her footsteps slow and deliberate, each one a question she was still learning to answer. The congregation watched her approach — Carmen from the laundromat, Ellis the mechanic, the barista from Dekalb, the woman whose keys had fallen from her hand on the bridge three days ago. They had all heard her frequency through the water, through the pipes, through their own bones. But they had never heard her voice.

She stopped at the edge of the group. The East River moved behind her, carrying the signal downstream toward the harbor.

"I hear you," she said.

The words hung in the air, simple and impossible. Because she did hear them — not just their voices, but the shape of every heart that had followed the frequency to this place. Every fluttering pulse, every held breath, every question the city had learned to swallow.

Carmen stepped forward. "I felt your song in every pipe in my building," she said. "I thought I was losing my mind."

Ellis nodded. "The rhythm came through my wrenches. I couldn't stop working. The music kept me going."

One by one, they spoke. Each fragment of the frequency found its way back to her — not as a broadcast anymore, but as a conversation.

"Do you feel it?" Folana asked them. "Not just in your ears. In your bones?"

And one by one, they nodded.

The frequency had found its body. And for the first time, the body could ask questions. For the first time, the body could answer back.
