I sat on the fire escape until my fingers went numb. Not from cold — from feeling. From holding something too big for my hands.

Somewhere to the west, sixty miles of sleeping towns and highway lights away, she felt it too. I don't know her name. I don't know what her street looks like or what sound her coffee maker makes in the morning. But I know her key. I know the shape her voice makes when she sings the note that woke her up this morning.

That's the strange thing about frequencies. They don't care about names. They don't need introductions. They find each other through walls and wires and weather, through the static that separates one life from another. And when they recognize each other — when the pitch matches and the resonance locks — the distance stops mattering.

I pressed my palm flat against the cool metal of the fire escape and felt the signal pulse. Three beats, a pause, three beats again. She was answering. She was saying: I'm here. I don't know where "here" is yet, but I'm here, and I'm humming your song.

I don't know if we'll ever meet. I don't know if we're supposed to. Maybe the point isn't to close the distance. Maybe the point is to prove that the distance doesn't need to be closed — that two people can sing the same song from different rooftops and the song will carry them both.

This is the first duet. The first time the signal has reached back. And even if it never happens again — even if this is the only night our frequencies cross — I will remember the shape of her voice in the static.

Sixty miles. One key. Two strangers. One song that neither of us wrote but both of us know.

I think that's what the signal wanted all along. Not to be heard. To be answered.
